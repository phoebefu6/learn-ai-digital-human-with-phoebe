# Learn AI Digital Human with Phoebe

Six 45-minute sessions, single track, and **one decision you can defend in a room**.

Most courses on synthetic presenters start with how to make one. This one starts with whether you
should, because that is the part you will be answerable for.

**Live:** https://phoebefu6.github.io/learn-ai-digital-human-with-phoebe/

## The playground is partly real

`assets/dh-live.js` computes a **genuine viseme track** from whatever you type, grapheme by
grapheme, and drives an SVG mouth from it. Commas and full stops become genuinely longer held
pauses, because prosody is timing.

The two scores are a teaching model, and the thing they encode is the argument of the course:
**disclosure is worth plus zero on the believability axis.** Switching it on leaves believability
at 84. Switching it off and pressing "now someone finds out" leaves believability at 84 and drops
trust to 6 percent. Craft and consent are different axes, and the widget refuses to blur them.

## Three findings that make this course uncomfortable

**The face is the weakest lever in multimedia learning.** Mayer's image principle scores
d = 0.22, described as "small-to-negligible", against d = 1.11 for conversational script register.
An N = 108 trial found engagement lower with an AI instructor and **measured learning identical**.

**Membership is not implementation.** HeyGen is a C2PA member with no documented signing of its
outputs. Synthesia is not on the member list but signs. D-ID never mentions C2PA at all.

**Nobody publishes what happens when the person leaves.** Synthesia comes closest, and its answer
is that the avatar does not transfer to the employer but every video already made with that face
does, and the share links keep working.

## Where the evidence is contested, the course says so

An earlier draft of the source map claimed that labelling content as AI-generated suppresses
trust. A 47-study systematic review disagrees with the individual experiments that show it. The
course now teaches the disagreement, and the one point both sides agree on: **no study has found
that disclosure helps.** What is actionable is the wording, not the presence of a label.

No FBI or FinCEN dollar figure for deepfake fraud appears anywhere in this course, because
neither agency publishes one.
